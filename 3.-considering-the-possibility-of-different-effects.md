# Considering the possibility of different effects

Users of medical literature often struggle to differentiate between the impact of prognosis (prognostic factors, risk factors, baseline risk) on the magnitude of absolute treatment effects (risk difference) and impact of subgroup effects (effect modification or subgroup analysis) on the magnitude of risk differences.

Differences in baseline risk—the risk without the intervention of interest— may be associated with patient or disease characteristics (e.g. old versus young). For instance, if the frequency of an adverse outcome is twice as great in the older versus the younger, the absolute effect or risk difference associated with an effective treatment will be twice as great in the older versus the younger.

On the other hand, a subgroup effect occurs when a particular patient or disease characteristic modifies the relative effective of a particular intervention. This means that the relative effect of a therapy differs between one group of patients and another. For instances, the relative risk reduction may be 50% in the elderly and only 25% in younger individuals.

The table below provides an exercise that has helped people distinguish between the impact of differences in baseline risk versus subgroup effect (differences in relative effect).

The three scenarios present hypothetical results of three randomized trials (A, B, and C) comparing a treatment group to a control group on a patient-important adverse outcome of interest (e.g. mortality).

Scenario A (different baseline risks, the same relative effects, different absolute effects across subgroups): In scenario A the baseline risk (control event rate) differs between the two groups: 40% in the old, 20% in the young. The relative risk is, however the same in two populations (10%/20% or 0.5 in the young; 20%/40% or 0.5 in the old). The risk difference is 10% in the young (20% - 10%), and 20% in the old (40% - 20%). Thus, this presents an example of constant relative risk, but differences in prognosis influencing the final risk difference.

Scenario B (different baseline risks, different relative effects and absolute effects across subgroups): In scenario B the baseline risk (control event rate) differs between the two groups: 40% in the old, 20% in the young. The relative risk also differs in the two populations (10%/20% or 0.5 in the young; 40%/40% or 1.0 in the old). The risk difference is 10% in the young (20% - 10%), and 0 in the old (40% - 40%). Thus, this presents an example of differences in both relative risk and differences in prognosis influencing the final risk difference.

Scenario C (the same baseline risks, different relative effects, different absolute effects across subgroups): In scenario C the baseline risk (control event rate) is the same, 20%, in the young and old. The relative risk, however, differs (10%/20% or 0.5 in the young; 20%/20% or 1.0 in the old). The risk difference is 10% in the young (20% - 10%), and 0 in the old (20% - 20%). Thus, this presents an example of constant baseline risk, but differences in relative risk influencing the final risk difference.

Scenario A is by far the most common. Prognosis often differs between risk groups such as the young and the old, or the sicker and the less sick. Relative effects are most often similar between these groups with different prognosis.

Scenario B is the next most common, but quite unusual. Groups differ in prognosis but also differ in the magnitude of the treatment effect.

Scenario C in which prognosis is the same in two groups of patients but relative risk differs is extremely unusual.

The following presents an example of Scenario B in which both the baseline risk and the relative risk differ between subgroups. The Study to Prospectively Evaluate Reamed Intramedullary Nails in Patients with Tibial Fractures (SPRINT) trial evaluated the use of alternative regimens for managing tibial factutes.<sup>1</sup> The researchers randomized patients into two groups: one in which the bone marrow was reamed and a large intramedullary nail inserted, and the other receiving unreamed nailing. The authors chose the frequency of reoperation or autodynamization as the primary outcome.

The primary analysis failed to detect a convincing difference between groups, with a relative risk reduction of 10% for reamed versus unreamed nailing (relative risk 0.90; 95% confidence interval 0.71 to 1.15). In a pre-planned subgroup analysis, the researchers hypothesized that reaming would be more beneficial in closed fractures while unreamed nailing would be better for open fractures. For patients with closed tibial fractures, reamed nailing lowered the risk by 33% (relative risk 0.67; 95% confidence interval 0.47 to 0.96), while for patients with open tibial fractures, reamed nailing increased the risk by 27% (relative risk 1.27; 95% confidence interval 0.91 to 1.78) (Table 2).

The statistical interaction test for this subgroup analysis showed a p-value of 0.01. This subgroup analysis was one of five that the researchers investigated. Using the ICEMAN tool,<sup>2</sup> we can rate the credibility of this subgroup effect as moderate (Table 3).

In regard to baseline risk, patients with open fractures had a greater risk of the primary outcome (29%) that did patients with closed fractures (11%). Therefore, fracture type (open versus closed) serves as both a prognostic factore (open fractures with a worse prognosis) and an effect modifier (reaming better for closed factures, non-reaming beter for open fractures) (Table 2).
