# 16. Alternative approach to rating up certainty of evidence from NRSI

Up to now, guidance from the GRADE Working Group has taken one of two approaches to assessing certainty of evidence from non-randomized studies of interventions (NRSI):

* Given the seriousness of the residual confounding problem, all NRSI start as low certainty evidence but can be rated down to very low or be rated up to moderate or high. This approach is valid, familiar to most GRADE users, and therefore we opted for recommending it as the primary approach in Core GRADE.
* Using ROBINS-I,<sup>1</sup> starting all NRSI as high certainty evidence.<sup>2</sup> The complexity of ROBINS–I is inconsistent with the objectives of Core GRADE.

However, in discussing this topic, we found ourselves also acknowledging that the primary GRADE position of always considering NRSI to start as low certainty has been one of the aspects of GRADE creating barriers to its adoption, particularly in fields where RCTs are more difficult to perform and therefore RCT based evidence less common, including public health, nutrition, toxicology, and rare diseases. This was one of the motivations for the creation of ROBINS–I and the enthusiasm of some GRADE working group members for its use.

Therefore, we have conceived and will present here an alternative conceptual framework to the process of rating up certainty of evidence from NRSI. This approach might appeal particularly to systematic reviewers, guideline developers and health technology assessors mostly relying on NRSI, including routine clinical observation.

The key difference between the primary approach presented in the main text and the alternative approach presented here is that the alternative one uses considerations about large effect and dose-response gradient not to rate up the certainty, but to establish the starting point for the certainty of evidence from NRSI. That is, RCT will always start at high certainty, and NRSI will start low unless they present a large effect or a dose-response gradient. Specifically, NRSI presenting a large effect (RR >2.0 or <0.5) or a gradient will start at moderate certainty. NRSI presenting a very large effect (RR > 5.0 or <0.2) or presenting any large effect and a dose-response gradient at the same time will start at high certainty. Thereafter, one will determine whether rating down certainty is needed or not by assessing risk of bias with criteria appropriate for RCTs or NRSI.

There are potential upsides to this alternative approach. First, it follows a more linear reasoning. Considering NRSI starting low in all cases, only to later modifying our judgment and recognize some are actually providing higher certainty may be perceived as a convoluted and contradictory process. One might consider it more logical and appropriate to set the starting level certainty looking at the characteristics of the study, beyond the simple aspect of randomization. This approach does not, as in ROBINS–I, suggest allowing liberally setting the certainty. However, it does use the large effect and dose-response gradient concepts not to “rate up” after rating down, but to set an appropriate starting point, in a somewhat more respectful and fair consideration of the quality and value of those specific studies and their authors. It is in the nature of the effect to be large or to present a gradient, and that strengthens the corresponding evidence, which therefore obviates the need to set a low certainty starting point.

Second, it takes away an unresolved and somewhat embarrassing situation which happens with the rating up mechanism, which is the decision to rate up (or not) after having rated down the same evidence during the RoB assessment. On this point, GRADE is not proposing a unique solution, leading to the same body of evidence potentially being assigned a different level of certainty depending on one rating up or not after having rated down. By using the large effect and gradient criteria to set the starting certainty point, the issue is naturally solved, as rating down will happen at a later step, with no ambiguity.

Third, the two criteria were empirically discovered. They are not common but present from time to time. Setting the starting level of certainty basing the judgement on empirically established criteria is a logical approach, which is very similar in nature to the concept of target trial,<sup>3</sup> or the assumption behind the logical construct of ROBINS-I.

We anticipate that those systematic reviewers, guidelines users and health technology assessors sympathetic with ROBINS-I but hesitating because of its complexity will find the alternative approach more natural and congenial. We are not suggesting, at this time, that in keeping with ROBINS–I, NRSI will start high or moderate for reasons different from large effects or gradient. However, should empirical evidence be put forward to make the case that other study characteristics might contribute to increase our certainty, they could be incorporated in future guidance.

Fourth, it is important to outline that the proposed alternative approach will lead exactly to the same results as the rating up process, thus not causing any disagreement or deviation in the appraisal of the same body of evidence.

The major downside we see to this approach, which we considered important enough to propose it as an alternative and not as the main approach, is the risk that it would appear as a significant deviation from current GRADE practice, and would therefore be unwelcome to long standing GRADE users. Also, we do feel that, while many may perceive the alternative approach as promising, organic, and logical on the theoretical plane, it has not been piloted and has not survived yet the empirical test of repeated application.

For all those who will opt to adopt the proposed alternative approach, we suggest they do mention it explicitly in their publications, Summary of Findings tables, and reports. Reporting on upsides and downsides experienced in applying the approach will help establishing the value of this alternative approach.&#x20;
